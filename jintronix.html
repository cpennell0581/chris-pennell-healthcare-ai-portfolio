01 The Challenge

Jintronix had the technology, but the surrounding workflow was not yet strong enough to drive clinical adoption. Therapists were not using the tool consistently, which meant patients had little reason to build a habit around it.

Clinicians needed to prescribe and monitor therapy without adding work to a full day. Patients needed motivation connected to recovery, not novelty. Early research was direct: "I don't know which clinical objectives I can address" and "It takes too long to navigate." Those friction points were showing up in procurement conversations. The next phase had to be built around clinical workflow.

A research methodology built for a clinical setting, not a usability lab.
Standard usability testing did not fit. Patients were medically fragile and therapists were mid-session. We used contextual inquiry across three care settings, intercept interviews around shift transitions, and remote session-log analysis to find patterns across hundreds of real patient sessions.
02
Jintronix wireframes and prototype iterations
Early session flow wireframes. We tested timing, audio cues, and scoring as separate ideas before sending any of it into development.
03
A four-step prescription flow built around what therapists already knew: their patient, not the game library.
The original interface required therapists to know Jintronix game names before they could prescribe anything. The redesign replaced that with a guided workflow. Choose position, choose supervision level, choose a clinical objective, then pick from a filtered activity set. Therapists who had never used Jintronix before could build a complete prescription in under a minute.

Three calls that shaped the product

03 PM Judgment

Over seven years, the product had to balance patient engagement, clinical credibility, and market timing. These were the calls where research changed the roadmap instead of decorating it.

Decision 01 Therapist dashboard: prioritizing clinician visibility because patient adoption depended on prescription
The situation

Early research showed that patients used what therapists recommended. The original roadmap prioritized the patient app first, with clinician tooling later. That would have built engagement on a recommendation loop therapists could not see or influence.

My recommendation

Build clinician visibility in parallel with the patient experience. Therapists needed adherence and progress data before they could confidently prescribe Jintronix between sessions.

What happened

The therapist dashboard became one of Jintronix's strongest sales tools. Once clinicians could see adherence and progress, many became advocates. That referral network helped the platform grow to 500+ clients without paid acquisition.

Risk managed

In a prescriber-dependent model, the clinical side had to earn trust first. Without that, patient engagement would depend on behavior the product could not influence.

Decision 02 Cut social comparison: giving up a short-term usage lift to protect clinical credibility
The situation

Pilot data showed leaderboards and peer benchmarking increased session engagement. The numbers made a real case for keeping them, but clinical advisors flagged a deeper risk: comparing recovery across different diagnoses was inappropriate and difficult to defend in enterprise sales conversations.

My recommendation

Cut social comparison and shift motivation to personal progress. The short-term engagement lift was real, but it was not worth the clinical and procurement risk.

What happened

Engagement dipped slightly at first. Hospital and senior care partnerships became easier to close because the product could defend its clinical model. We gave up a consumer-style metric to protect enterprise credibility.

Risk managed

Keeping leaderboards would have created clinical liability and blocked hospital and senior care procurement conversations. A measurable engagement lift was worth less than enterprise credibility in the target market.

Decision 03 Scale ahead of polish: moving when three hospital networks were ready to sign
The situation

After two years, Jintronix had strong pilot data and known rough edges. Sales had three hospital networks ready to sign, and competitive pressure was increasing. Waiting for a cleaner release risked losing the window.

My recommendation

Scale with existing partners while addressing rough edges in parallel sprints. I separated functional blockers from cosmetic gaps so leadership could move with a clear risk profile.

What happened

All three hospital networks signed that quarter. The risk stratification held. None of the cosmetic issues became functional blockers in the new settings. Waiting would have let the window close.

Risk managed

Separating blockers from polish gave leadership a defensible basis to move. Waiting for a cleaner release would have sacrificed real market momentum.
