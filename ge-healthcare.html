01 The Problem

The project started in the field because GE Healthcare had a throughput problem, and throughput was a business problem. If a scan room lost minutes to avoidable software work, the scanner lost revenue. GE put the cost at roughly $2.3M in lost utilization per machine each year. We observed technologists across 9 hospitals in 6 countries to see where the workflow actually broke down.

The pattern became clear in the room. Technologists were managing hidden state, inconsistent tools, and a worklist that moved slower than the patient queue. They were trying to care for the patient and keep the exam moving while the console kept forcing them to re-orient themselves.

The fieldwork produced more than 80 pain points across 8 workflow stages. Those findings became a 57-page interaction specification for the AW Server convergence program, covering the major surfaces from worklist behavior to series navigation.

04 The Ideal Experience Model

Before we designed screens, we translated the research into an ideal experience model. The question driving all of it was this. If the software worked the way technologists needed it to work, what would a good shift feel like? And what would the product have to do to create that feeling? We used it to test proposed designs against what technologists had actually asked for.

How the model was built

We grouped the field observations around one question. What does a successful shift feel like to a technologist? Four answers kept coming back. Fast, autonomous, supported, reassuring. Each feeling was paired with the product qualities required to produce it. What the user feels on the left. What the software has to be on the right.

05 The Interaction Design

How the research changed the design

01
The toolbar had grown by accretion. The redesign had to reduce visual competition without slowing expert users down.

Experts had learned to ignore most of the interface. Newer technologists were overwhelmed by it. The redesign started from what we saw in the room: make the workspace easier to read without taking speed away from experienced users.

Before redesignDense controls and competing modes made the workspace hard to read at a glance.
After redesignThe same workspace, simplified into a clearer hierarchy that surfaced state faster.
02
Before screen design, the research had to establish the product model.

The existing product had grown without a clear organizing model. Fieldwork made the split obvious: Browser was for finding and preparing; Viewer was for acquiring and assessing. Locking that distinction into the architecture gave every later screen decision a stable product logic.

AW Server interaction architecture - product structure before individual screen concepts were designed
03
A small set of tools carried most scan work. Finding the patient before the exam was often harder than running the exam.

Across sites, 80–90% of toolbar use came from a handful of functions. Grouping tools by frequency and context shortened the expert path and made the toolbar more legible for newer technologists. In the browser, slow worklist search repeated across a full day became a throughput problem, so progressive filters kept the query running while the technologist narrowed criteria.

Viewer toolbar: four groups organized by frequency and context of use
Browser search: progressive filtering that kept queries running in the background

04
Series navigation was a high-friction moment that needed a structural fix, not a cosmetic cleanup.

Moving between series took too many clicks, and the default viewport often showed the first localizer instead of the most recent acquisition. Technologists had to hunt for their place in the exam. The series navigator gave that state a permanent home, with floating and docked modes to support different throughput environments.

Viewer system grid - the spatial framework for the series navigator, toolbars, and parameter panels. Floating and dockable behavior preserved flexibility across throughput contexts.
AI opportunities
Adaptive image review
The viewer was designed to surface state clearly. An AI layer could flag probable motion artifacts, coverage gaps, or quality exceptions as they appear, giving technologists a chance to fix issues before the patient leaves the table.
Predictive series setup
The series navigator exists because prescription copying is the dominant workflow. A prediction layer could pre-fill likely parameter adjustments from anatomy, prior acquisitions, and site patterns, so the fastest setup path is also safer.
Intelligent worklist triage
Browser search was designed to run queries in the background. AI ranking by urgency, equipment state, or exam context could take prioritization work out of the technologist's head.

07 Key Research Findings

What the fieldwork clarified

01
The highest-leverage friction appeared before the scan started.

The pattern held across all six countries. Too much time burned on setup: finding the right patient, locating prior exams, adapting protocols, and entering data the scanner should already have known. In high-throughput sites, that overhead was the difference between a smooth day and a backlog that compounded all afternoon.

Browser behavior, worklist logic, search, and pre-populated data became the front door to the exam. Every minute saved in setup was a minute the scanner could actually run.

78 scans observed across all 9 hospitals
02
Automation was welcome when it preserved clinical control.

Technologists wanted pre-populated patient data, smart protocol defaults, and faster setup for routine cases. When concepts removed too much manual involvement, experienced technologists pushed back. They wanted help with predictable work, not removal from judgment calls.

The design rule was simple: automate the predictable parts and keep technologists in control of decisions that required expertise.

4 persona types, each with distinct automation needs
03
Patient reassurance was a scan-quality issue, not a hospitality detail.

The technologists who got the best images spent time making patients feel safe before the bore. A calm patient holds still. A still patient produces a cleaner image. A cleaner image reduces rescans and protects the schedule.

That changed how the team treated patient-facing features. Welcome information, education screens, and positioning guidance affected image quality and throughput, not just patient comfort.
