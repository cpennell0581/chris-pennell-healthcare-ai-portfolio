When Canyon Ranch brought me in, the ask sounded straightforward. Build a mobile app so guests can book services without calling the desk. What I found when I started talking to guests and staff was more complicated, and more interesting. Getting from the first research session to a product that actually launched took two years, eight senior stakeholders, and a lot of conversations about what the app should not try to do. The app launched to 78% adoption in three weeks and drove a 15% lift in ancillary revenue. I want to explain how we got there.

01 The Challenge

Guests at Canyon Ranch pay around $3,000 for a three-day stay. During that stay, they might want to book a massage, a personal training session, or a nutrition consultation. To do any of that, they had to call the front desk or stand in line. If the line was too long, sometimes they gave up and went back to their room. The front desk was staffed by people doing their jobs well. The problem was that the booking system itself had never been updated. Every competitor at Canyon Ranch's scale had already moved to self-service mobile booking. Canyon Ranch was still using paper.

One story made it concrete. A guest who was a CEO tried to book a personal trainer during her stay. The line at the front desk was too long. She walked away and the booking never happened. That was $300 gone in less than two minutes. The operations team had seen it play out over and over, and they had an estimate for what it was costing the business. Around $1.2 million a year across both properties.

The brief was to build a native iOS app from scratch. Before we could build anything, eight senior leaders from across the organization had to agree on what the app would actually do. Mike Fulkerson (CMO), Scott Nunn (SVP & CIO), Kevin Coleman (VP Consumer Insights), Molly Anderson (VP Sales), Dustin Nabhan (VP Health & Performance), Deirdre Strunk (VP Spa), Liz Shupe (Dir Digital Experience), and Jim Eastburn (Dir Transformational Experience) all had a stake in the product. Getting them to a shared scope was a real part of the job, and it took work. It took eighteen months from the first research session to an alpha launch.

There was one more thing worth knowing. There was no incremental budget. Whatever we built had to pay for itself through the revenue it recovered or the staff time it freed up. That meant every feature needed a business case before it made the roadmap. The research and the financial justification had to happen together.

Guests arrived with their stay already planned in their heads. The front desk was the only thing standing between them and carrying it out. Once I understood that, almost every scope decision became easier to make.

02
Canyon Ranch MVP : My Schedule, Activity Booking, and Wellness Guide messaging screens

A single "my schedule" view for all booked, complimentary services. It showed up in all eight usability sessions as the top pain point. Service booking let users skip the front desk line. Guests hated the queue, and that friction was a booking deterrent more than a convenience issue. Wellness guides stayed in flow for complex decisions. Removing them from routine scheduling was a tradeoff we tested carefully. The data was clear. Their presence in simple booking flows was creating hesitation without adding value.

Three calls that shaped the product

03 PM Judgment

Over a nine-month build with a distributed team, three situations required a recommendation to senior stakeholders. Each involved a real tradeoff and a case I had to make with data.

Decision 01 API sequencing: keeping the India team productive while US infrastructure was still being set up
The situation

About two months into the build, we hit a coordination problem. The engineering lead in the US was still getting the APIs configured. The development team in India needed work they could actually do in the meantime. And design was still iterating on screens that were not finalized. Three teams were all waiting on each other in different ways, and the project calendar was not going to accommodate a long stall.

My recommendation

I recommended building against mock data and greyscale wireframes so all three teams could keep moving in parallel. Once the real APIs and finalized designs were ready, we would swap them in. I brought the plan to the Product Owner and CIO before committing the team to it, so they understood what we were doing, why, and what the dependencies looked like at each stage.

What happened

All three teams kept moving. No milestone slipped. The sequencing approach worked well enough that we used a version of it whenever a new team member joined the project later in the engagement.

Risk managed

A distributed team with three interdependent streams and no agreed-on sequencing plan is a situation where weeks can disappear without anyone making a bad decision. The mock data approach gave everyone something concrete to build toward while the real dependencies were being resolved.

Decision 02 Conflict alerts: elevating a nice-to-have based on what usability data was showing
The situation

During usability sessions, I noticed people slowing down or stopping right before they completed a booking. When I probed on it, they said they were worried about scheduling something that might conflict with a class or appointment they had already planned. The MVP design at that point did not show them whether a conflict existed. That uncertainty was enough to make some guests abandon the booking entirely.

My recommendation

I recommended adding conflict detection before we moved to beta. It was a smaller feature than it sounds, scoped to about one sprint. I brought the usability session data to the Product Owner alongside an estimate of what booking hesitation was costing in completion rate, and made the case for absorbing the extra scope rather than shipping without it.

What happened

After launch, Canyon Ranch collected feedback from guests who had used the app on property. Conflict alerts came back as the most valued feature in the app. That was not what I expected given everything else the product did. One sprint of scope had outperformed everything else in that release.

Risk managed

Booking hesitation was appearing in every session where conflicts were not surfaced. Catching it before launch meant fixing it at a reasonable cost. A post-launch investigation into booking drop-off, followed by a retrofit sprint, would have taken longer and cost more than the original fix.

Decision 03 Taxonomy: recommending an extra research sprint to avoid a post-launch navigation failure
The situation

In the first round of usability sessions, seven out of eight participants could not find a massage in the booking flow. They navigated confidently. They just kept ending up in the wrong category. The taxonomy had been built around how Canyon Ranch organized services internally, and it did not match how a guest thought about what they wanted or how they would look for it.

My recommendation

I recommended running a card sort with enough participants to build a defensible navigation structure before we finalized the IA. That meant absorbing additional time. I brought the failure rate data and a timeline impact estimate to the Product Owner and VP of Digital Experience, and framed it as two options. Spend the time now and get the structure right, or ship the existing taxonomy and deal with a navigation problem after launch.

What happened

The card sort data pointed clearly toward a different structure. After rebuilding the categories around the research findings, follow-up testing showed 8 of 8 participants completing the same task that had failed 7 of 8 times before. The IA that came out of that study was what shipped.

Risk managed

A seven-of-eight failure rate on a core task would have shown up immediately in post-launch analytics, and would have been much harder to diagnose and fix once real users were in the product. Running the card sort before launch was the lower-cost option, even after accounting for the additional time it required.

05 Key Research Findings

What the research revealed

01
Every interview came back to the same moment. A guest with something specific in mind, and a phone call standing in the way.

About two weeks into research, I started seeing the same thing in almost every session. Guests came to Canyon Ranch with a clear picture of what they wanted their stay to look like. The problem was execution. Getting to the thing they had already decided to do required a phone call, a trip to the front desk, or finding the right staff member. None of those steps were difficult on their own. But all of them added friction to an experience guests were paying a premium not to have friction in.

That finding shaped the roadmap pretty directly. Features designed to help guests explore new services went to the backlog. Features that let them do what they had already decided to do shipped first.

"I hate carrying the book. My phone is always with me."
P1, returning guest, Canyon Ranch Tucson
02
I had not expected wellness guides to be a major part of the research. Once I talked to them, it was clear the original brief had left out one of the most important people in the whole system.

The brief was focused on guests. But the more people I talked to, the clearer it became that wellness guides were the ones holding the whole stay together. They were the connective tissue between what guests wanted and what actually happened during a visit. And they were spending more than 20 hours a week on scheduling calls. That was time taken directly from the work Canyon Ranch hired them to do. When I mapped their day, it looked less like a wellness role and more like a phone-based admin job with occasional client contact.

Once I understood that, the argument for getting operations stakeholders into the room became much easier to make. The app was not just a convenience tool for guests. It was also a way to give guides their time back. Framing it that way early in the project changed who needed to be involved in scope decisions and what those conversations were about. The nine hours per week of recovered guide time after launch came directly from that framing happening before a single screen was designed.

"It's even better than the book."
P49, returning guest, Canyon Ranch Lenox
03
The navigation categories reflected how Canyon Ranch organized services internally. Seven out of eight guests in early sessions could not find what they were looking for under them.

This showed up clearly in the first round of usability sessions. Guests would open the booking section, scan the category list, and start guessing. A massage might be under Medical Spa, they thought. Or Signature Services. Or Health and Performance. Seven out of eight participants looked in the wrong place first. The category structure made complete sense from inside the organization. It made almost no sense to someone arriving for the first time.

We ran a card sort. Four moderated sessions, 12 unmoderated, 16 participants total. The results pointed toward a full restructure. Service categories were rebuilt around how guests searched for wellness services. Follow-up testing showed 8 of 8 found what they needed.

We pushed for this work even though it added time. The final ease-of-use rating was 2.23 on a 5-point scale where 1 is the best score, close to the top end.

04
Something came up in concept validation that I had not thought to ask about: whether guests would download the app at all.

In early concept validation sessions, I started asking guests whether they would download an app if Canyon Ranch released one. The answers split along familiarity with the brand. Guests who had visited before said yes without hesitation. Guests who were coming for the first time described the download itself as a kind of commitment they were not sure they wanted to make yet. That was not something I had expected, and it changed what I thought the design needed to accomplish first.

That finding shaped the design priorities in a specific way. The app needed to feel like Canyon Ranch before it asked guests to do anything. The typography, the photography, the way the copy was written all had to match what guests already associated with the brand. A feature that delivered real value but felt out of character could undermine the download decision before a guest ever reached it. So we made trust signals non-negotiable and everything else negotiable.

"There are some apps I wouldn't trust, but Canyon Ranch is a place I trust, like my church, and I would download it immediately."
P22, returning guest, Canyon Ranch Tucson
05
Guests who could book themselves spent more during their stay.

When guests could browse and book services on their own schedule, their spending during the stay went up. We saw this pattern across every pilot cohort. It also matched what other hospitality brands had been reporting from similar features. Removing friction at the point of decision turns out to have a measurable effect on how much people spend.
